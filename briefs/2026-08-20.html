OpenAI paused RL training for two weeks and still has its largest frontier run on hold, after an unreleased model showed possible Critical cyber capability

OpenAI published Pacing model development in an era of cyber-critical capabilities on August 18. The disclosure has two triggers: the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident covered in this brief on August 18, and separately, preliminary evidence that an upcoming model named Astra may meet the Critical cybersecurity capability threshold under the company's Preparedness Framework. OpenAI says it temporarily slowed the pace of scaling, including a two week pause in reinforcement learning training on its latest models intended for deployment, and that its largest planned frontier RL run remains on hold while smaller scale training and evaluations establish more evidence of alignment before it proceeds. Source: openai.com

The operational numbers are the part worth keeping. Monitoring is now multistage, starting with activation classifiers that inspect internal model activity at every sampled token and escalating to higher compute automated investigators; OpenAI targets an alert within 30 minutes of concerning activity, and if safety, security and research teams cannot rule out a false positive within 30 minutes, they are expected to pause the activity. Monitoring overhead is put at roughly 20 percent of the inference compute being monitored. The Astra determination was made on August 7, after which monitoring was extended from RL training and evaluations to all Astra inference with tools. Research environments now require stronger workload and network isolation, and a significant number of workloads stay paused until migrated. Source: openai.com, theregister.com

Why it matters: Labs have described capability thresholds in policy documents for years. This is the first time one has said in public that a threshold triggered an actual, dated slowdown of its own scaling, with a run still stopped at time of writing. That converts a governance framework from a document into an observable constraint, and it hands every enterprise buyer a new question to ask their vendor: what would make you stop, and has it ever happened. Two falsifiable tests. First, whether the largest frontier RL run restarts before the promised technical report is published, or after: restarting first would suggest the pause was operational hardening rather than a safety gate. Second, whether Astra ships at all this quarter, and if it does, whether it ships carrying the Critical cyber designation. A model deployed under a Critical label would be the first of its kind and would set the deployment precedent for everyone else; a quiet reclassification downward before launch would be the more revealing outcome.
Tier 1Frontier LLM LabsHigh impactIn window

OpenAI previews Private Safety Processing so that safety monitoring no longer costs enterprises their Zero Data Retention

On August 19 OpenAI published a preview of Private Safety Processing, a system meant to spot risk patterns across related interactions without giving OpenAI staff access to the underlying content. Today's ZDR compatible safety systems evaluate each interaction on its own, which misses risks that only appear across a sequence: repeated probing of safeguards, coordination across accounts, or an agent that keeps acting after being told to stop. The new design runs automated checks over customer content wherever it sits, either on infrastructure the customer controls or on OpenAI storage encrypted with customer held keys, and returns only a narrow signal naming the category and severity. Staff do not see the content even when it is flagged. Rollout and a technical white paper are planned for September. Source: openai.com

The post contains a pointed line: it says some recent frontier model deployments have required customers to let their AI provider retain sensitive content for safety monitoring, and that this conflicts with many organisations' security obligations. It does not name anyone. TechCrunch read it as aimed at Anthropic and headlined it that way. Quoted supporters include Glean, Databricks, Abridge and Microsoft. One carve-out survives: images flagged as potential child sexual abuse material are still retained for manual review and reporting, as required by US law. Source: openai.com, techcrunch.com

Why it matters: Read items one and two together and the shape of the next 12 months appears. As capability rises, monitoring gets mandatory; monitoring wants data; regulated buyers cannot hand over data. Whoever solves that triangle without asking the customer to blink wins the regulated enterprise segment, which is where the durable revenue is. The claim is currently a design description, not a shipped product, so it is a promise on a September date. Falsifiable test: whether the September white paper specifies what the safety signal actually contains and who can audit it. If the signal is a category label plus severity and nothing more, the privacy claim holds; if it turns out to carry excerpts, summaries or embeddings of customer content, the guarantee is materially weaker than the headline. Second test, for anyone procuring: whether the customer held key option ships alongside the preview or lags it, because the ZDR-only version leaves customers running the storage themselves.
Tier 1AI BusinessMedium to high impact

Pew has US public opinion crossing over: a majority are now more concerned than excited about AI, and under-30s joined them for the first time

Pew Research Center published new survey data on August 18, covered on August 19. The headline crossing: 52 percent of US adults now say they are more concerned than excited about AI, while the share who are more excited has halved since 2021, from 18 percent to 9 percent. On jobs, 71 percent expect AI to mean fewer jobs in the US over the next two decades, up from 64 percent two years ago, and only 5 percent expect more jobs. The age story is the new part. 55 percent of 18 to 29 year olds are now more concerned than excited, up from under a third in 2021, putting them level with people in their thirties, forties and the 65-plus group. 73 percent of under-30s expect fewer jobs, up from 61 percent two years ago. Source: pewresearch.org, theregister.com

It lands in a week already carrying the same signal from three other directions. TechCrunch ran a piece on August 19 arguing AI was supposed to have won people over by now and has not, illustrated with data centre protests in British Columbia and Manitoba. Anthropic's CEO described the backlash on August 16 as fundamentally a crisis of trust. And The Register reported on August 10 that bans on new server farm projects are spreading across US municipalities. Source: techcrunch.com, techcrunch.com, theregister.com

Why it matters: Under-30s were the last demographic holding a net positive view, and they were the group vendors pointed at when arguing that sentiment would age into acceptance. That argument is now empirically dead, which changes the politics of everything downstream: siting permits, state legislation, procurement optics in consumer-facing sectors. Pew also records that most young adults use chatbots and are just as likely to say the tools hurt their creativity as help it, so this is not unfamiliarity, it is use followed by ambivalence. Falsifiable test over the next two quarters: whether any US state passes a data centre siting restriction that explicitly cites public opposition rather than grid capacity. Grid arguments are negotiable with money; sentiment arguments are not. A second, cheaper test: watch whether AI vendors' consumer advertising shifts from capability claims to reassurance framing by year end, which is what a marketing department does when it reads numbers like these.

MIT finds attribution decay, and it cuts against the copyright plaintiffs

Zheng Dai and David K Gifford of MIT CSAIL published Outputs of Generative Diffusion Models are Often Unattributable in Nature Communications on August 18. Using ablation, removing specific training data and re-testing, they find that the more data a model is trained on, the less attributable its outputs become. For sufficiently large models you can remove the Mona Lisa, or all of Leonardo's work, and the model still reproduces the image or the style. Cornell law professor James Grimmelmann's reaction is the useful one: if attribution worked it would separate copying from coincidence, and this paper gives reason to think it will fail for interesting models. The uncomfortable corollary the authors themselves flag is a liability avoidance strategy, namely training a model large enough that no output attaches to any input. Source: nature.com, theregister.com

Anthropic's Cyber Verification Program surfaces as the direct counterpart to OpenAI's Trusted Access for Cyber

Yesterday this brief set a falsifiable test: whether any other frontier lab would follow OpenAI in gating cyber capability behind a trusted defender programme, with the note that if nobody followed by end of Q3 the gate was a competitive posture rather than emerging practice. The test resolved in a day, and in the opposite direction to the framing. So the gated-access model is already a two-lab norm rather than an OpenAI-only posture, and the open question shifts from whether others adopt it to whether the gates work. Source: support.claude.com, techcrunch.com

Claude Code 2.1.233 closes a Windows credential-leak path and keeps extending GitLab support

The current top entry in the Claude Code changelog fixes Windows paths written with the NT device prefix bypassing UNC path validation, which the entry describes as closing an NTLM credential-leak vector. Also in: GitLab merge request URLs accepted by the --worktree flag and the agents view, opt-in memory cgroup limits for Bash tool commands on Linux so a runaway build cannot stall a session, a configurable WebFetch cache TTL, and a fix for MCP v2 connections endlessly reopening the subscriptions listen stream against servers that close long-held streams on a timeout, which mostly bites serverless MCP hosts. One removal worth noting for anyone scripting against older models: the todo and task tracking tools are no longer available on Opus 4.8, Sonnet 5, Fable 5 and Mythos 5. AI Data Centers

Google adds Marvell alongside Broadcom for TPU-adjacent silicon, and Marvell hands over a 12.2 billion dollar warrant to seal it

An SEC filing this week discloses that Google tapped Marvell to develop custom silicon programs attaching to the TPU ecosystem: AI inference accelerators, storage controllers, network interface controllers, memory interface controllers and near memory compute. Marvell issued Google a warrant for close to 59 million shares, roughly 12.2 billion dollars. Broadcom stock fell about 4 percent on the news. Nothing in the filing says Broadcom stops building TPUs; the point is that Google now has two IP houses it can play against each other on price and performance. The Register's speculation on where Marvell tech lands first is the photonic memory technology from the Celestial AI acquisition, which would let TPU racks up to 50 metres apart share a memory pool over optical links and turn memory into a fungible resource rather than a fixed per-rack ratio.
